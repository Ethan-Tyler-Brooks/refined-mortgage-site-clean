Not Married and Buying a House Together in Wisconsin

Ethan Brooks · Mortgage Advisor, NMLS #1639987 · 7 min read

A lender does not care whether you are married. Wisconsin’s property rules do. You and your partner can buy a house together without a marriage license, share one mortgage, and both land on the deed — but almost nothing a married couple gets handed to them happens for you by default. It has to be written down at closing, while everyone is still getting along.

I get this call a few times a month, from couples who have the savings and assumed the hard part would be the loan. The loan is the easy part. What bites later is the deed, the exit, and a Wisconsin fee nobody warned them about.

Can an unmarried couple buy a house together in Wisconsin?

Yes, and the mortgage side is unremarkable. Federal lending law bars a lender from treating you differently because of marital status, so two unmarried co-borrowers get underwritten exactly the way a married couple does. Both incomes count, both credit reports get pulled, and both sets of debts land in the ratios.

What changes is everything around the loan. Wisconsin’s marital property rules apply to married couples, and they are why a spouse here often ends up with rights in a home without doing anything. None of that machinery reaches an unmarried couple. The upside is that you are not pulled into each other’s debt the way a married applicant can be — this is the mirror image of buying a house without your spouse in Wisconsin. The downside is that no default protects either of you, so silence at closing is a decision, not a deferral.

Whose income and credit does the lender actually use?

Both, if you both go on the loan — two incomes buy more house than one. The catch is credit: most lenders qualify and price a joint application on the weaker of the two credit profiles, not the average. One thin or bruised file quietly sets the terms for both of you.

There is a second structure worth pricing: put only the stronger borrower on the mortgage and both names on the deed. The lender underwrites one person, so one income and one set of debts, but both of you are legal owners from day one. You trade borrowing capacity for a cleaner qualification, and it is worth running both ways before you pick. A conventional loan in Wisconsin is the usual home for this, because its guidelines are the most flexible about who holds title versus who holds the note. If this is a first purchase for either of you, look at the Wisconsin first-time homebuyer options at the same time.

One thing to keep straight: the deed says who owns the house, the note says who has to pay for it. You can be an owner without being a borrower, but you cannot stop being a borrower just because you moved out and signed the deed over. Removing a name from a mortgage takes a refinance or a lender’s written release.

How should the deed read if we are not married?

This question deserves an hour and usually gets ten seconds. A deed can be written so the survivor takes the whole property if one of you dies, or so each share passes instead to that person’s own will and heirs. Very different outcomes for the same house, and the deed wording decides which one you get. Do not assume a default that favors you.

Unequal contributions are the other thing to settle in writing. If one of you brings most of the cash and the other covers the renovation, a deed that simply names you both says nothing about it. Title companies draft what they are told to draft. An attorney and a short written agreement — who paid what, who pays monthly, what happens if one of you leaves — settles arguments that otherwise end up in court.

What happens if one of us wants out later?

Here is the Wisconsin-specific cost nobody mentions at the start. When a married couple splits, a deed between spouses, or between ex-spouses as part of a divorce, is exempt from the state’s real estate transfer fee. Unmarried partners do not get that exemption. Wisconsin does exempt conveyances between registered domestic partners, but the Department of Revenue only allows it where the declaration was filed on or before April 1, 2018, and the registry has been closed to new declarations since. For everyone else, buying your partner out is a taxable conveyance like any other.

The math is small but real. The Department of Revenue sets the fee at 30 cents for each $100 of value, or fraction of $100. Say you own a home valued at $320,000 together and one of you deeds a half interest to the other: the value conveyed is $160,000, so the transfer fee is $480, due when the deed is recorded. A married couple doing the identical thing pays nothing. Add a refinance to get one name off the note and the real exit price is well past $480 — which is why it belongs in a conversation before you buy, not after.

What should we settle before we write the offer?

Four things, none of which take long. Decide who goes on the loan and get a preapproval that reflects it. Decide how the deed will read, and tell the title company in advance rather than at signing. Write down who contributed what and what happens if one of you leaves. And get clear on who carries the monthly obligations, because the lender pursues whoever signed the note no matter what the two of you agreed between yourselves.

None of this is a reason to hesitate. Unmarried couples buy houses in Wisconsin every week and it works out fine — because somebody asked these questions early, when they were cheap to answer.

Buying together and want it structured right?

Fifteen minutes is usually enough to map out who goes on the loan, what it does to your numbers, and what to tell the title company.

Frequently asked questions

Can an unmarried couple get a mortgage together in Wisconsin?

Yes. Lenders cannot treat you differently because of marital status, and two unmarried co-borrowers are underwritten the same way a married couple is: both incomes count, both credit histories are reviewed, and both debts count in the ratios. The difference is not the loan, it is the paperwork around it. Wisconsin’s marital property rules apply to married couples, so nothing about ownership or survivorship happens for you automatically and the deed has to say what you intend.

Does one partner’s weaker credit affect a joint mortgage in Wisconsin?

It usually does, because most lenders qualify and price a joint application on the weaker of the two credit profiles rather than the average. One option is to put only the stronger borrower on the mortgage while both names go on the deed, which keeps both of you as legal owners. That trades borrowing capacity for a cleaner qualification, so it is worth pricing both structures before you choose.

Do unmarried co-owners pay a Wisconsin transfer fee to buy each other out?

Usually yes. Wisconsin exempts conveyances between spouses and between ex-spouses in a divorce, and it exempts conveyances between registered domestic partners whose declaration was filed on or before April 1, 2018. Unmarried partners outside that window get no exemption, so the transfer fee applies to the value of the interest being conveyed. A transfer return has to be filed with the deed either way.
