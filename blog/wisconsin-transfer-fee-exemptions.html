Wisconsin Transfer Fee Exemptions: When No Fee Is Due

Ethan Brooks · Mortgage Advisor, NMLS #1639987 · 6 min read

In Wisconsin the seller pays the real estate transfer fee, which is why most buyers never think about it — right up until the deal is not an ordinary sale. Buying the family cottage from your parents, taking an ex-spouse off a deed, inheriting a house, finishing a land contract: in each of those the fee may be zero. Sorting out which exemption applies before the deed is drafted is far easier than fixing it afterward.

What is the Wisconsin transfer fee, and who actually pays it?

Wisconsin charges a fee when real estate changes hands. State law puts it on the grantor — the party giving up the property, which in a normal sale is the seller — at 30 cents for every $100 of value, or fraction of $100. The Department of Revenue's filing guide does the arithmetic as value multiplied by .003.

So on a $325,000 sale, the transfer fee is $975. The county register of deeds collects it when the deed is recorded, and it lands on the seller's column of the settlement statement. As a buyer you are not paying it, which is why it goes unnoticed until you are in a transfer where nobody is sure whether a fee is owed.

It is collected through the electronic Real Estate Transfer Return — the RETR — filed alongside the deed. That return is where an exemption gets claimed.

Which transfers are exempt?

Every exemption sits in one statute, Wis. Stat. § 77.25, and the Department of Revenue publishes a guide listing each one with its conditions. Much of that list covers corporate mergers, partnership conversions and government takings. These are the ones that come up in ordinary life:

  • Close family, for nominal or no consideration — parent and child, stepparent and stepchild, parent and son-in-law or daughter-in-law, grandparent and grandchild.
  • Between spouses, and between ex-spouses in a divorce.
  • By will, descent or survivorship — the deed that follows a death, including a termination of a decedent's interest. A separate exemption covers a nonprobate transfer on death, and a document filed only to name a transfer-on-death beneficiary needs no return at all.
  • Into a trust, where the same transfer made directly to the trust's beneficiary would itself be exempt.
  • Property worth $1,000 or less.
  • Foreclosure or a deed in lieu of foreclosure, where the property returns to the mortgage holder or a land contract seller.
  • A deed finishing a land contract, if the proper fee was paid when the land contract was recorded.
  • A corrective deed that confirms or fixes a conveyance already on record, for nominal or no consideration.
  • Between a limited liability company and its members, when every member is closely related and nothing changes hands beyond debt.

What is the catch on family transfers?

This is where people get caught out. The family exemption works only when the consideration is nominal or nothing. A true gift of the home qualifies. A sale to your daughter at a friendly discount does not, at least not entirely.

The Department of Revenue handles that middle ground as part sale and part gift, and allows a partial exemption: the fee is owed on the portion that is genuinely a sale and excused on the portion that is a gift. How the split is reported matters, so have that conversation with the closing attorney or title company before the deed is drafted.

One thing this does not settle. Transfer fee treatment and mortgage underwriting are separate questions. If your parents sell you a home below market value, the difference is usually documented as a gift of equity, and a lender has its own rules for sourcing and papering that — see how gift funds work on a Wisconsin mortgage.

Does an exemption mean no paperwork?

No, and this is the most common misunderstanding. Nearly every exempt conveyance still needs a transfer return filed with the deed. What changes is that you select an exemption number instead of paying.

The state's electronic system is strict about it. Based on how you describe the document type, the conveyance type, and who the grantor and grantee are, it offers only the exemption numbers your answers qualify for. If the one you were counting on does not appear, that is not a glitch — it is the system telling you the exemption does not apply and the fee is due. Two narrow cases skip the return entirely: conveyances recorded before October 1, 1969, and a document filed solely to designate a transfer-on-death beneficiary.

Where does this touch a normal purchase?

Buying from family. The deed may be exempt, but you are still getting a mortgage, still being underwritten, and still needing an appraisal. Start with the Wisconsin first-time homebuyer guide if this is your first purchase, and expect a lender to look harder at a non-arm's-length sale than a listed one.

Divorce. Moving a house between ex-spouses is exempt from the fee. It does not remove anyone from the loan — that takes a refinance or a release from the lender. The two get conflated constantly; see whether a spouse has to sign the mortgage in Wisconsin.

Paying off a land contract. The fee should have been handled when the contract was recorded. If it was, the deed that follows is exempt. If nobody can find that record, expect a call to the register of deeds.

Recording in Milwaukee County. Filing practice varies county to county. If you are buying in Milwaukee or the surrounding suburbs, let the title company confirm local procedure rather than assuming.

The bottom line

In a straightforward purchase the transfer fee is the seller's problem and the exemption list is trivia. The moment a deed moves between relatives, through an estate, out of a divorce, or at the end of a land contract, it stops being trivia: the fee is often zero, but only if the right exemption is claimed with the right description of the transfer. For the fee itself explained from the ground up, start with the Wisconsin real estate transfer fee.

Frequently asked questions

Who pays the real estate transfer fee in Wisconsin?

The grantor pays it, which in an ordinary sale means the seller. State law sets the fee at 30 cents for each $100 of value or fraction of $100, and the county register of deeds collects it when the deed is recorded. It appears on the seller's side of the settlement statement, so a buyer in a normal arm's-length purchase does not pay it.

Is a transfer between a parent and child exempt from the Wisconsin transfer fee?

It can be, but only when the consideration is nominal or nothing at all. The exemption covers parent and child, stepparent and stepchild, parent and son-in-law or daughter-in-law, and grandparent and grandchild. If the child pays real money for the home, the Department of Revenue treats it as part sale and part gift, and the fee is due on the part that is a sale.

Do you pay a transfer fee when you add a spouse to the deed in Wisconsin?

No. Conveyances between spouses are exempt, and so are conveyances between ex-spouses handled as part of a divorce. A transfer return still has to be filed with the deed, and being exempt from the fee does not take anyone off the mortgage, which is a separate matter handled by the lender.
