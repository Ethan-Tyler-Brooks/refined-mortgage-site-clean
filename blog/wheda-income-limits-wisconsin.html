WHEDA Income Limits in Wisconsin: What Actually Counts

Ethan Brooks · Mortgage Advisor, NMLS #1639987 · 7 min read

The question I get asked about WHEDA more than any other is some version of do we make too much? It is a fair question with a frustrating answer, because WHEDA does not measure your income the way your lender does. There are two different income numbers in a WHEDA file, and the one that decides eligibility is usually the larger of the two.

Plenty of buyers talk themselves out of WHEDA on bad arithmetic, and a few get to an accepted offer before anyone catches that the household is over. Both are avoidable. Here is how the limits work, taken from WHEDA’s own published table.

Whose income does WHEDA actually count?

Everyone who will live in the home. WHEDA calls the figure total household compliance income, and that phrasing is deliberate: it is not household income in the loose sense and it is not the qualifying income on your application. It is the gross income of every adult occupant, counted whether or not that person is a borrower, signs the note, or has any connection to the loan at all.

In practice the person who moves the number most often is a non-borrowing spouse. There are good reasons a couple might put only one spouse on the loan — credit, self-employment history, debt that would sink the ratios. Leaving a spouse off the application does not leave their income out of the WHEDA test.

The same goes for a working adult child or a parent moving in. If they occupy the home and have income, it counts. WHEDA has its own worksheet for the calculation, filled out separately from the underwriting file.

Why is there more than one income limit?

Because WHEDA runs several programs and they do not share a ceiling. Your limit turns on which program, which county, and how many people are in the household.

  • First-time home buyer, MCC and VALOR — the tightest limits of the set.
  • WHEDA Advantage conventional and FHA — noticeably more generous, and you do not have to be a first-time buyer to use them.
  • Household size — every limit has a one-to-two-person figure and a higher figure for three or more.
  • Target areas — designated neighborhoods inside certain cities carry higher limits, and inside them the first-time buyer requirement is waived entirely.

One more column on WHEDA’s table is not an eligibility limit at all, and it causes real confusion: the figure labelled 80% of area median income exists only to set mortgage insurance coverage on a conventional file. Being over it disqualifies nobody.

What are the limits in my county?

Under the table effective June 13, 2026, a one or two person household in Milwaukee, Waukesha, Washington or Ozaukee County can earn up to $108,400 and still use the first-time home buyer program, or up to $124,660 on WHEDA Advantage conventional or FHA. Three or more people in the household lifts those to $124,660 and $146,340.

Dane County is the highest in the state at $135,300 for one or two people, and a target area inside Madison goes to $162,360. Kenosha County sits at $117,300, its city target areas at $140,760. Racine County and much of rural and northern Wisconsin share a $105,800 baseline, and many northern counties are target areas in their entirety, lifting the figure to $126,960 and dropping the first-time buyer requirement.

Those numbers move. WHEDA reissues the table every several months, and the version in force is the one dated for your registration, not the one you read last spring. Treat any figure online, this page included, as a starting point and confirm the current table before you write an offer. The WHEDA loan programs we work with in Wisconsin all run off it.

Is there a price limit on the house too?

There is, and it is a separate test. For the first-time home buyer, MCC and VALOR programs, the purchase price limits effective June 13, 2026 are $566,364 for a single unit and $725,146 for a two to four unit property in a non-target area. Inside a designated target area those rise to $692,222 and $886,290.

For most Wisconsin buyers this cap never comes into play. The income test is the one that decides.

What does this look like on a real Wisconsin file?

An example: a married couple buying their first home in Waukesha County. He earns $72,000 and applies alone, because her student loan balance would strain the ratios. She earns $43,000 and will live in the home. His qualifying income is $72,000 — comfortably under anything. Their total household compliance income is $115,000, which is over the $108,400 first-time buyer limit for a one-or-two-person household in that county, and under the $124,660 WHEDA Advantage limit. The first-time buyer program is out. WHEDA Advantage conventional is still available, with down payment assistance attached. Nobody has to walk away — but the question gets answered before the offer, not after.

What if the household is over the limit?

Then WHEDA is not your path, and that is fine. A household over the ceiling is, by definition, a household with income — which opens up conventional and FHA options carrying no occupancy covenant for the life of the loan and no recapture acknowledgement at closing. Being over a WHEDA limit is not a rejection; it is a reason to compare the first-time buyer programs available in Wisconsin on their merits.

Worth knowing either way: a WHEDA loan requires the home be owner-occupied for the life of the loan, the property must be in Wisconsin, and first-time buyers must complete pre-purchase home buyer education.

The bottom line

WHEDA eligibility turns on total household compliance income — everyone who lives there, borrower or not — against a county limit that varies by program, household size and target-area status. If you are anywhere near the line, run the household figure before you fall for a house. The down payment assistance a WHEDA loan carries is worth the ten minutes.

Frequently asked questions

Whose income counts toward the WHEDA income limit?

The whole household's. WHEDA tests total household compliance income, which is the gross income of every adult who will live in the home, including a spouse who is not on the loan. That is the single most common reason a file that looks comfortably under the limit on the loan application turns out to be over it. It is a different number from the qualifying income your lender uses to approve the loan, and it is calculated on its own WHEDA worksheet.

What is the WHEDA income limit for my Wisconsin county?

It depends on your county, your household size and which WHEDA program you use. Under the limits effective June 13, 2026, a one or two person household buying in Waukesha, Washington, Ozaukee or Milwaukee County can have total household compliance income up to $108,400 for the first-time home buyer program, or up to $124,660 for WHEDA Advantage conventional and FHA. Dane County runs higher at $135,300, and designated target areas inside cities like Milwaukee and Madison are higher again. WHEDA publishes the full county table and updates it periodically, so check the current version before you rely on a figure.

Is there a limit on the price of the house with a WHEDA loan?

Yes, and it is separate from the income limit. For the first-time home buyer, MCC and VALOR programs, the purchase price limits effective June 13, 2026 are $566,364 for a single unit and $725,146 for a two to four unit property in a non-target area, rising to $692,222 and $886,290 inside a designated target area. Most Wisconsin buyers are nowhere near these numbers, so the income test is almost always the one that decides eligibility.
