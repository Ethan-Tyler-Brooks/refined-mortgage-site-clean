Removing an Ex-Spouse From a Mortgage in Wisconsin

Ethan Brooks · Mortgage Advisor, NMLS #1639987 · 7 min read

The judgment is signed, the house is yours, and you assume that settles it. Then a mortgage statement arrives with your former spouse’s name still printed on it. In Wisconsin the deed and the note are two separate pieces of paper, and a divorce judgment only reliably moves one of them.

This is the most common surprise I see on the far side of a Wisconsin divorce, and it usually turns up months late — when someone applies for a car loan and finds a mortgage they thought they were rid of.

Doesn’t the divorce judgment take my ex off the mortgage?

No, and the reason decides what you do next. A divorce judgment is an order between you and your former spouse. The mortgage is a contract between both of you and a lender that was never a party to your divorce. A Wisconsin family court can order your ex to deed you the house, and can order you to be solely responsible for the payment, and your lender remains entitled to collect from anyone who signed the note.

The consequences cut both ways. The spouse who moved out stays liable for a debt they no longer control, and it keeps landing on their credit report and inside their debt-to-income ratio when they try to buy their own place. The spouse who kept the house has a co-borrower whose credit can be damaged by a late payment they had nothing to do with. The judgment protects neither of you from the lender.

What does the deed actually do, then?

It moves ownership, and that part works cleanly. A quitclaim deed from your former spouse to you, recorded at the county register of deeds, takes their name off title. After it records you own the house outright on paper — and you both still owe the money. Title and debt are separate questions in Wisconsin, the same split that surprises people going the other direction, when they add a spouse to the deed and discover it does nothing to the loan.

Sequence matters here more than almost anything else. If your ex-spouse signs the deed and then moves three states away, you hold the title and are stuck with their name on the note and no cooperation available to remove it. Plenty of Wisconsin attorneys will hold the signed deed until the new loan is ready to close, or write the judgment so the conveyance and the payoff land in the same transaction. Ask for that deliberately.

The gap, in one example: a Wauwatosa house worth about $310,000 with $188,000 left on the loan. The judgment awards the home to one spouse, the quitclaim deed records, and the title is clean in a single name. That $188,000 note still carries two names, shows up on two credit reports, and creates two sets of liability — until a new loan replaces it or the house sells.

Do we owe the Wisconsin transfer fee on the deed?

Generally not. The Department of Revenue’s published exemption guidance treats a conveyance between husband and wife as exempt from the real estate transfer fee, and is explicit that the exemption reaches former spouses: the conveyance has to be coded as a divorce or spousal transfer, the relationship shown as spouses or ex-spouses, and both parties must be individuals rather than a trust or an LLC.

Read the conditions rather than the headline. The exemption is from the fee, not the filing — a Wisconsin real estate transfer return still goes to the Department with the deed, claiming it, and the register of deeds wants that receipt before anything is recorded. The county recording fee is due either way. Our rundown of Wisconsin transfer fee exemptions covers the neighbouring cases.

How do I actually get an ex-spouse off the loan?

  1. Refinance. You apply on your own income and credit, and the new loan pays off the old one. This is the normal answer and the only one fully inside your control. A Wisconsin refinance is a complete application — income documents, appraisal, title work — and whether it is a good trade depends heavily on the loan you are giving up. We lay that out in when refinancing is actually worth it.
  2. Assumption, or a written release of liability. Some loans can be assumed, and some servicers will release one borrower while the loan stays in place. Both need the servicer’s written approval and your own qualification, and plenty of loans permit neither — but it costs one letter to ask. See assumable mortgages.
  3. Sell. If neither of you can carry the house alone, selling ends the liability for both of you in one transaction — often the honest answer when the numbers do not work on one income.

What if I cannot qualify on my own?

Court-ordered maintenance or child support can count as qualifying income once there is a documented history of receiving it and enough remaining term, which is exactly why the wording and dates inside your judgment matter to a loan officer. A co-signer is sometimes the bridge, and a refinance that buys out a former spouse’s interest is not always treated like an ordinary cash-out.

What does not help is waiting quietly. Price the refinance before the judgment is final, so the deadline written into the settlement is one you can actually meet and the fallback — usually a listing requirement — is in writing before anyone needs it.

The bottom line

A Wisconsin divorce judgment can award you the house and order who pays for it. What it cannot do is rewrite a contract your lender signed. The deed takes a name off the title; only a new loan, an approved assumption, or a sale takes a name off the debt. Work out which you need while both signatures are still in the room.

Frequently asked questions

Does a Wisconsin divorce decree remove my ex-spouse from the mortgage?

No. A divorce judgment is an order between the two of you; the mortgage is a contract with a lender that was not part of your divorce. The court can award you the house and order you to pay the loan, and the lender can still collect from anyone who signed the note. Until the loan is refinanced, assumed with a written release, or paid off in a sale, both names stay on the debt and on both credit reports.

Do we owe the Wisconsin transfer fee when a deed moves the house in a divorce?

Generally no. The Wisconsin Department of Revenue's exemption guidance for conveyances between husband and wife reaches ex-spouses, provided the transfer return is coded as a divorce or spousal conveyance, the relationship is shown as spouses or ex-spouses, and both parties are individuals. The exemption is from the fee and not from the filing, so a transfer return still goes to the Department with the deed. The county recording fee is still due.

Can I assume the existing mortgage instead of refinancing?

Sometimes, and it is worth asking before you assume the answer is no. FHA and VA loans are often assumable, and some servicers will formally release one borrower from liability on a loan that stays in place. Both routes need the servicer's written approval and your own qualification, and plenty of conventional loans allow neither. Ask your servicer in writing, keep the answer, and treat a refinance as the plan until they tell you otherwise.
