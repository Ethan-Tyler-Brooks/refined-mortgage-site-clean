How to Add Your Spouse to the Deed in Wisconsin

Ethan Brooks · Mortgage Advisor, NMLS #1639987 · 7 min read

You closed in your name alone — maybe their credit needed work, maybe the timing was wrong, maybe you were not married yet. Now you want both names on the house. In Wisconsin that is a short document and a flat recording fee, and the thing most people worry about is the thing that does not happen.

This lands in my inbox a few times a month, usually from someone who read our piece on buying a house without your spouse in Wisconsin and did exactly that. They assume it will be expensive, or that the lender will object, or that it quietly makes their spouse responsible for the mortgage. Here is what actually happens.

Doesn’t my spouse already own part of the house?

Possibly, and this surprises people. Wisconsin is a marital property state, so property either of you acquires during the marriage is generally marital property regardless of whose name appears on the paperwork. A home you bought before the marriage is a different animal, and so is one bought with traceable premarital money.

What marital property does not do is put your spouse on the recorded title. The register of deeds reflects the deed, not the marital property act, and the deed is the record a title company, a buyer, a refinance lender or a probate court reads. Wisconsin’s homestead rules are also why your spouse may have signed paperwork at your closing without being an owner — we unpack that in does my spouse have to sign the mortgage.

What document actually adds a spouse to the deed?

A new deed — most often a quitclaim deed — from you as grantor to you and your spouse as grantees. Wisconsin married couples commonly take title as survivorship marital property, which passes the home to the survivor without probate, but that choice has estate and tax consequences, so it is worth a short conversation with an attorney rather than a guess on a blank form.

Two things travel to the county register of deeds: the deed itself, and a Wisconsin real estate transfer return filed electronically with the Department of Revenue. Recording without the return is not an option — the register of deeds wants the transfer return receipt before the deed goes on record.

The dollar figure people ask for: recording a deed in Wisconsin costs a flat $30, regardless of how many pages the document runs. That is the whole recording cost of adding your spouse. Drafting it properly is the expense worth paying for — the recording is not.

Do I owe the Wisconsin transfer fee on this?

No. The Department of Revenue treats a conveyance between spouses while married as exempt from the real estate transfer fee, and the exemption is broad enough to cover a gift, a sale or a land contract between the two of you. On the transfer return the conveyance gets coded as a spousal transfer, with both of you shown as individuals.

Read that exemption precisely, though. It is an exemption from the fee, not from the filing, and it turns on being married at the time of the conveyance: an unmarried couple deeding a half interest to each other does not get this treatment, and neither do ex-spouses settling up outside the divorce judgment. Our rundown of Wisconsin transfer fee exemptions walks through where the lines fall.

Will my lender call the loan due?

Not for this. Nearly every mortgage contains a due-on-sale clause that lets the lender demand payoff if the property changes hands, and federal law carves out a specific list of transfers the lender cannot use that clause against. A transfer in which the borrower’s spouse or children become an owner of the property is on that list, for a home the borrower occupies or will occupy.

You do not need permission, in other words. You do want to notify your servicer once the deed is recorded, so their records match the title, and to keep a recorded copy. If the property is a rental rather than your residence, have someone read the note first.

Does this make my spouse responsible for the mortgage?

No, and this is the misunderstanding that costs people the most. The deed and the note are separate documents doing separate jobs. The deed says who owns the house. The note says who promised to pay for it. Recording a new deed moves the first and leaves the second exactly where it was.

The practical result is lopsided on purpose: your spouse becomes a legal owner with no obligation to make a payment, and you stay the only person the lender can pursue. For most couples that is fine. If what you actually want is both names on the debt — to share the credit history, or because one of you is the stronger earner now — the only route is a new loan you both sign. That is a Wisconsin refinance, and whether it makes sense depends entirely on the loan you are giving up.

When waiting is the better move

Two situations where I tell people to hold off. If a refinance or a move is already on the calendar, fold the deed change into that transaction instead of doing it twice. And if your spouse carries collections, judgments or liens, putting them on title can attach those claims to the house — a question for an attorney before you record, not after.

The bottom line

Adding your spouse to a Wisconsin deed is one of the cheapest pieces of paperwork in real estate: a properly drafted deed, a transfer return claiming the spousal exemption, a flat recording fee, a note to your servicer. What it does not do is change your loan. Know which of those two you were trying to fix and the decision gets simple.

Frequently asked questions

Does adding my spouse to the deed in Wisconsin trigger the transfer fee?

No. The Wisconsin Department of Revenue treats conveyances between spouses while married as exempt from the real estate transfer fee under sec. 77.25(8m), and that covers a gift, a sale or a land contract between you. You still file a real estate transfer return with the deed claiming the exemption, because the exemption is from the fee, not from the filing. What you actually pay is the county recording fee.

Does adding my spouse to the deed put them on the mortgage?

No, and this is the part people get wrong most often. The deed and the note are separate documents. Recording a new deed changes who owns the property; it does nothing to the loan, which stays in the name of whoever signed the note. Your spouse becomes an owner with no obligation to pay, and you remain the only person the lender can look to. The only way to put both names on the debt is to refinance into a new loan you both sign.

Can my lender call the loan due if I add my spouse to the title?

Not for this. Federal law lists transfers where the borrower's spouse or children become an owner of the property among the transfers a lender may not use to enforce a due-on-sale clause, at 12 U.S.C. sec. 1701j-3(d)(6), for property the borrower occupies or will occupy. Tell your servicer after the deed is recorded so their records match the title, and keep the recorded copy. If your loan is an unusual product or the home is not your residence, have your advisor read the note first.
