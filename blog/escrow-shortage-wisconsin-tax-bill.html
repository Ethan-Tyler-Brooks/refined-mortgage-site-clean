Escrow Shortage After Your First Wisconsin Tax Bill

Ethan Brooks · Mortgage Advisor, NMLS #1639987 · 6 min read

You closed in the summer, the payment was set, and nothing about your loan has changed since. Then December arrives, your municipality mails its tax bill, and your servicer writes to say your payment is going up. Nothing went wrong. Your escrow just met your real tax bill for the first time.

This is the most common why did my payment change call I take in January, and it lands almost entirely on people who bought in the previous twelve months. Here is why it happens, what your servicer may do, and what to check before January 31.

Why does the first tax bill blow up an escrow account?

When your loan closed, nobody knew what your tax bill would be. The lender had to estimate it, and the only figure available was the tax on the property as it stood for the seller — their assessment, their credits, last year’s levy. If you paid more than the last assessment reflected, if the municipality revalued, or if the seller held a credit you do not qualify for, that estimate was low before you signed a thing.

Escrow is not built to absorb that. Under the federal rules your servicer collects one twelfth of what it expects to pay out each year, plus a reserve that cannot exceed one sixth of those expected annual payments. A reserve that size covers timing, not a bill that came in materially higher than anyone forecast. When the real number shows up, the account is short.

Your statement names which of two things you have. A shortage means the balance is below target but still positive. A deficiency means it went negative, usually because your servicer advanced its own money to pay the taxes on time. The rules differ for each.

When does the Wisconsin tax bill actually land?

Later than most buyers expect, which is a large part of why this catches people. Wisconsin law requires the clerk to distribute property tax bills no later than the third Monday in December (sec. 74.09(5), Wis. Stats.). Under the state’s default schedule the first installment is due by January 31 and the second by July 31, though a municipality can enact its own schedule and some allow more than two installments.

So a buyer who closed in June sees no real tax figure for six months, and neither does the servicer. The analysis that resets your payment follows within weeks of the bill: the servicer must run one at the end of each escrow computation year and send an annual escrow statement within 30 days of that year ending.

Check one thing now rather than in February: the bill has to reach you. State law requires the municipality to mail it to the taxpayer or a designee, and where that designee is your servicer, it is supposed to pass you a copy. If you bought this year and nothing arrives by the end of December, call the municipality and confirm it has you on record as owner. Not receiving it does not excuse late payment, and late Wisconsin taxes carry interest of 1 percent a month back to February 1, with some counties adding a penalty. Around Milwaukee that is a different treasurer in every suburb.

How much can the payment actually change?

Two things stack, which is what surprises people braced for one. The going-forward deposit rises to cover your real annual tax bill — a permanent change reflecting what the house costs to carry. Then the shortage has to be made up. The federal rules leave the servicer limited room: where the shortage is at least one month’s escrow payment, it may either leave it alone or require repayment in equal monthly instalments over at least twelve months. Where it is smaller than one month’s payment, it can also ask for it within 30 days. So part of the increase falls away after a year, and part of it does not.

What does this look like on a real Wisconsin file?

An example. A buyer closes on a Wauwatosa house in June. The closing figures leaned on the seller’s last tax bill of $5,400, so the account was sized for roughly that. The property had been revalued, and the December bill arrives at $7,200 — $1,800 more a year than planned for. The servicer raises the deposit to cover $7,200 going forward and recovers the $1,800 the account is behind, over twelve months. A year later the catch-up disappears; the higher deposit does not. Nobody erred at closing — the seller’s bill was the only number in existence.

Can you keep it from happening again?

Partly. You cannot make a municipality bill you sooner, but you can remove the surprise.

  • Ask what tax figure your file used before closing and compare it to the price. A wide gap is your warning.
  • Check the credits you qualify for are on the bill. They attach to owner-occupants, and the first bill after a sale is where they get missed.
  • Read the escrow statement, not just the new payment. It itemises the catch-up, so you can see what is temporary.
  • Offer to pay the shortage outright. Most servicers accept it even though they cannot demand it that way.

If the arithmetic still does not work once the catch-up ends, that is a different conversation — and the moment to understand how Wisconsin property taxes and escrow fit together.

The bottom line

An escrow shortage in your first winter as a Wisconsin homeowner is not a billing error or a sign you were sold the wrong loan. It is the gap between the seller’s tax bill and yours, surfacing the week the clerk mails the real one. Expect it, read the statement, and remember part of the increase expires. Frequently asked questions

Why did my mortgage payment go up when my loan terms never changed?

Almost always escrow, not the loan. Your principal and interest are fixed by your note; the taxes and insurance collected alongside them are not. When the real tax bill from your municipality comes in higher than the estimate used at closing, your servicer has to collect more going forward and also make up the gap that has already opened. The note itself is untouched. Your annual escrow statement shows both pieces separately.

Can I just pay the escrow shortage in one lump sum?

Usually yes, and it is worth asking. The federal escrow rules set what a servicer may require, not what it must accept, and most servicers will apply a lump sum to the shortage if you call and ask. Paying it off removes the twelve month repayment piece from your payment, but it does not lower the going forward escrow deposit, because that reflects your actual tax bill. Ask for written confirmation of how the payment was applied.

What if my Wisconsin tax bill never arrived after I bought the house?

Call your municipality and confirm it has you on record as the owner. Wisconsin requires the municipality to mail the bill to the taxpayer or to a designee, and a recent sale is exactly when that record goes wrong. Not receiving the bill does not relieve you of the obligation to pay the tax or the interest if you are late. Unpaid Wisconsin property taxes accrue interest of 1 percent per month back to February 1, and some counties add a further penalty.
