Your First Wisconsin Property Tax Bill After Closing

Ethan Brooks · Mortgage Advisor, NMLS #1639987 · 6 min read

If you closed on a Wisconsin home this year, an envelope is coming in December with your name on it and a number on it that may be larger than you expected. It is your first property tax bill. Nothing has gone wrong — but there are three things on that bill worth understanding before you decide whether to worry about it.

This is the question I get most often from recent buyers in December. The bill is addressed to them rather than the lender, and it reads like a demand for money they thought was already handled. Usually it was. Here is how to read it.

When do Wisconsin property tax bills come out?

Wisconsin runs on a December calendar. State law requires the municipal clerk to distribute property tax bills no later than the third Monday in December, and the bill you receive that month is for that same tax year, not the year ahead. That trips people up, because the payment lands in the new year while the tax belongs to the old one.

Payment is due in full by January 31. Most municipalities also allow installments — under the default state schedule the first is due January 31 and the second July 31 — but local governments can set their own schedule, so the dates printed on your bill are the ones that matter. Miss them and the balance goes delinquent February 1, with interest at 1% a month back to that date, plus a county penalty in some places.

Do I pay this, or does my lender?

If you have an escrow account — and most buyers do — your servicer pays it. You have been paying one-twelfth of the estimated annual tax with every mortgage payment, and at closing the lender also collected several months of tax reserves precisely so the account could cover a bill that arrives before a full year of payments has gone by. The municipality still mails the bill to you as the owner of record; your servicer gets its own copy and pays from escrow.

That said, do not file it unread. The obligation to pay the tax stays with you as the owner, and failing to receive a bill does not excuse a late payment. In January, check your servicer's site to confirm the payment posted. And if no bill arrives at all by the end of December, call your municipal treasurer — after a sale, records sometimes still point at the previous owner.

Why doesn't the bill match what I saw at closing?

This is the real source of the surprise, and it has two common causes.

The first is simply that the figure used at closing was an estimate, usually built from the prior owner's most recent bill. If the assessment changed, or your municipality raised its levy, the real number moves.

The second is more specific to Wisconsin, and almost nobody sees it coming: the lottery and gaming credit. That credit reduces the bill, but it goes only to someone who owned the home and used it as their primary residence on January 1 of that tax year. If you bought in June, you did not own it on January 1, so the credit cannot appear on this year's bill. The seller had it; you do not get it until next year's bill. You claim it through your county treasurer — or your municipal treasurer if the home is in Milwaukee County, where buyers in Milwaukee and the surrounding suburbs file differently from the rest of the state.

An illustration. Suppose your closing estimate came from last year's bill of $4,900, which included the seller's lottery and gaming credit. This December your actual bill arrives at $5,400 — a modest assessment increase, plus a credit you are not yet eligible for. Escrow was funded against the smaller number, so the account comes up roughly $500 short across the year. Your servicer's annual escrow analysis catches that and resets the monthly escrow portion, usually spreading the shortfall over the following twelve months. Nothing is wrong; the estimate simply met reality. (Figures are illustrative only — your bill depends on your property, municipality, and assessment.)

Worth knowing: the seller already covered their part of the year. Wisconsin closings credit the buyer for the seller's share of taxes up to the closing date, which is why tax proration appears on your settlement statement. You pay the whole December bill, but you were handed money toward it at closing.

What should I actually check on the bill?

  • The name and parcel number — both should be yours, and the parcel should match your closing documents.
  • The credits line — the first dollar credit and school levy tax credit apply to most residential parcels and already reduce the amount due. The lottery and gaming credit may be absent for the reason above.
  • Any star or flag for unpaid prior-year taxes — rare after a clean closing, but if you see one, call the county treasurer rather than ignoring it.
  • The assessed value — if it looks high next to comparable homes nearby, note it. The window to challenge an assessment opens in spring, through your municipality's Board of Review, not in December.

The bottom line

Your first Wisconsin tax bill is usually a paperwork event, not a cash event: escrow pays it, the seller funded part of it, and the number looks unfamiliar mostly because a credit you have not earned yet is missing. Read it, confirm it is in your name, then watch for your escrow analysis — that statement, not the bill, is where a change to your monthly payment shows up. Frequently asked questions

When do Wisconsin property tax bills come out?

Wisconsin law requires the municipal clerk to distribute property tax bills no later than the third Monday in December, and the bill you get in December is for that same tax year. Payment is due in full by January 31, or in installments where your municipality offers them, with the first due January 31 and the second July 31 unless your local government has set a different schedule.

Does my lender pay the property tax bill out of escrow?

On most loans with an escrow account, yes. Your servicer collects one-twelfth of the annual tax with each payment, plus reserves gathered at closing, and pays the bill when it comes due. You should still read the bill when it arrives and confirm the payment posted, because the obligation stays with you as the owner even if the servicer misses it.

Why is the lottery and gaming credit missing from my first tax bill?

The Wisconsin lottery and gaming credit goes to the person who owned the home and used it as their primary residence on January 1 of that tax year. If you bought mid-year, you did not meet that test for the current bill, so the credit will not appear until the following year. You claim it through your county treasurer, or your municipal treasurer if the property is in Milwaukee County.
